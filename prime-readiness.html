Prime Readiness

A prime's first question about your shop isn't about your machines.

Before a prime — or the tier-one supplier above you — puts a shop on its bid list, it checks a set of gates. Cybersecurity is the one everyone talks about, and it is usually the biggest, but it is one of about six. What stops a shop first depends on what it makes and who it is selling to. So we start with a map: which gates apply to your shop, what you already have, what each one costs, and in what order to take them.

The gates

Six things a defence buyer checks, in the order they usually bite

None of this is about being bigger. It is about being answerable: a buyer needs to be able to show its own customer that every shop in its supply chain meets the requirements it passed down.

Registration: SAM.gov, UEI and CAGE

Free, and still the thing that most often holds a shop up. Registering puts you in the federal government's supplier system (SAM.gov), gives you the identifiers a buyer looks you up by, and a code called CAGE that primes ask for first. It renews every year — a lapse quietly takes you off the books until it is fixed.

Your cybersecurity position

Where controlled data lives, who can reach it, and the evidence that it is protected to the standard the contract names — NIST SP 800-171, with CMMC the version written into defence contracts. It is also a doorway: some of the other gates below will not open until this one is answered.

Export control (ITAR)

If you machine parts on the government's munitions list, or handle their drawings, you register with the State Department and renew annually — including a shop that never exports, because making the part is enough. The real work is naming who owns this, with a backup, and controlling how drawings move.

Quality

A certificate that says your shop writes down how it works and follows it — a general one, and for aerospace and defence work a stricter version that primes write into their supplier requirements. If you heat treat, weld, plate or inspect to a special process, that process needs its own accreditation too. Buyers check the certificate online, so an expired or out-of-scope one is worth nothing.

What rides in on the purchase order

Clauses come down with the order: protecting controlled data, keeping counterfeit parts out, tracing material, and — for metalworking above all — where the metal was melted. Certain alloys used in defence items have to be melted in the United States or a qualifying country, and that is settled before you quote, never after.

The buyer's own onboarding

Capability and quality questionnaires, a visit to the shop, and a place on its approved supplier list before a single order is placed. It also runs its own financial review — a loan approval is a strong signal, but it is not the buyer's test.

Which of these bite first depends on the part and the customer. A shop making non-controlled parts for a commercial customer may need only the first gate; a shop making flight hardware may need four.

Where we start

The map, before any workstream

We do not open with a compliance project. We open with the map, which is a small, fixed piece of work — and for most owners it is the first time anyone has told them plainly what is actually required of them.

What comes back

  • Which gates apply to your shop, and which do not
  • What you already have that counts — most shops have more than they think
  • What each remaining gate costs, in money and in hours, and who does the work
  • The order to take them, so nothing is done twice
  • Who owns each one — you, your IT provider, a specialist, or us

Why it comes first

Because the alternative is paying for work you did not need, or discovering the gate you missed while a bid is open. The map is also the honest way to price the rest: once we know which tracks apply, the hours are visible, and so is the fee.

It is a separate engagement from the financing, agreed in writing like everything else, and it stands on its own — you can take the map and do the rest yourself, with your own people or someone else.

Who does what

Paperwork and evidence, translated

Some of this is your IT provider's time, some is a specialist's, and some is simply a decision only you can make. Our job is to keep the whole thing moving and to keep the records.

We do

Questions

What owners ask about this

Is this just CMMC?

No. Cybersecurity is the gate people talk about most, and it is usually the largest, but it is one of about six. The map covers the rest: registration, export control, quality, the clauses that ride in on a purchase order, and the buyer's own supplier qualification.

Do I need all of it?

It depends on the part, the customer and the contract. Some shops need one gate, some need three. Working out which ones apply to your shop is exactly what the map is for, and it is deliberately the first thing we do.

What does it cost?

The fee is agreed in writing before any work starts, and it is fixed rather than a percentage of anything. The map is a small piece of work at a fixed fee. The larger tracks, if you need them, are priced afterwards against the hours they actually take.

Will this get me a contract?

No one can promise that, and anyone who does is selling you something. The prime still decides who it buys from. Being able to answer its requirements is what this buys: it makes your shop one a prime can consider, which is further than most small shops get.

Do I have to do this before I can get a loan?

No. They are separate engagements with separate fees, and neither depends on the other. A lender will ask where you stand on controlled data, so doing the map early can make the financing conversation easier — but the loan does not require it, and neither SBA nor any lender requires you to use us at all.
